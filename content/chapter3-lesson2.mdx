Lesson 2 of Chapter 3 develops **electromagnetic induction** (អាំងឌុចស្យុងអេឡិចត្រូម៉ាញេទិក) — the discovery by Michael Faraday (and independently by Joseph Henry) that a *changing* magnetic flux through a conducting loop induces an electromotive force (EMF). This lesson defines magnetic flux, states Faraday's law and Lenz's law with all four sign cases, derives the moving-rod EMF, introduces eddy currents, and closes with the electric generator and electric motor as the central engineering applications. Equations (១) through (៦) are defined here.

---

## 1. Magnetic Flux (ភ្លុចម៉ាញេទិក) — Equation (១)

**Magnetic flux** measures how much of the magnetic field passes perpendicularly through a surface of area $A$. For a uniform field $\vec{B}$ at angle $\alpha$ to the normal of the surface:

$$\boxed{\Phi_B = \vec{B} \cdot \vec{A} = BA\cos\alpha = B_\perp A} \tag{១}$$

**Symbol glossary:**

| Symbol | Meaning | SI Unit |
|--------|---------|---------|
| $\Phi_B$ | Magnetic flux | Weber (Wb = T·m²) |
| $B$ | Magnetic field magnitude | Tesla (T) |
| $A$ | Area of the loop or surface | m² |
| $\alpha$ | Angle between $\vec{B}$ and the area normal $\hat{n}$ | degrees or radians |
| $B_\perp$ | Component of $\vec{B}$ perpendicular to the surface | T |

**Three special cases:**

| Angle $\alpha$ | Flux | Situation |
|---------------|------|-----------|
| $0°$ | $\Phi_B = BA$ (maximum) | $\vec{B}$ perpendicular to loop plane |
| $90°$ | $\Phi_B = 0$ | $\vec{B}$ parallel to loop plane |
| $180°$ | $\Phi_B = -BA$ | $\vec{B}$ anti-parallel to normal |

## 2. Faraday's Law (ច្បាប់ផារ៉ាដេ) — Equations (២) and (៣)

Any change in magnetic flux through a conducting loop induces an EMF. The **average EMF** over time interval $\Delta t$ for a coil of $N$ turns is:

$$\boxed{\mathcal{E} = -N\frac{\Delta\Phi_B}{\Delta t}} \tag{២}$$

As $\Delta t \to 0$, the **instantaneous EMF** is:

$$\boxed{e(t) = -N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}} \tag{៣}$$

<Note>
The negative sign in Faraday's law is not merely a convention — it encodes Lenz's law (see Section 3). It tells us the direction of the induced EMF *opposes* the cause of induction.
</Note>

---

## 3. Lenz's Law (ច្បាប់ឡិនទី២)

Lenz's law states that the **induced current always flows in the direction that opposes the change in flux** that caused it. This is the physical content of the minus sign in Faraday's law.

The textbook systematically works through four magnet-motion cases:

| Case | Motion | Change in flux | Induced current opposes by… |
|------|--------|---------------|---------------------------|
| 1 | Magnet (N pole toward coil) approaching | $\Phi_B$ increasing | Creating an N pole facing the magnet → **repulsion** |
| 2 | Magnet (N pole) moving away | $\Phi_B$ decreasing | Creating an S pole facing the magnet → **attraction** |
| 3 | Magnet (S pole toward coil) approaching | $\Phi_B$ increasing (opposite sign) | Creating an S pole facing the magnet → **repulsion** |
| 4 | Magnet (S pole) moving away | $\Phi_B$ decreasing | Creating an N pole facing the magnet → **attraction** |

**TikZ figure in source:** The pull-the-magnet-away diagram (រូបទី២៦) illustrated at `0.95\linewidth` showing all four cases with field lines and induced-current arrows.

<Warning>
Lenz's law is a consequence of energy conservation: if the induced current *aided* the flux change instead of opposing it, we could extract unlimited energy from a magnet — which is impossible.
</Warning>

## 4. Moving Rod in a Magnetic Field — Equation (៤)

A conducting rod of length $l$ moves with velocity $v$ perpendicular to a uniform field $B$. As it sweeps out area $\Delta A = l \cdot \Delta x$ in time $\Delta t$:

$$|\mathcal{E}| = \frac{|\Delta\Phi_B|}{\Delta t} = \frac{B \cdot \Delta A}{\Delta t} = \frac{B \cdot l \cdot \Delta x}{\Delta t}$$

$$\boxed{|\mathcal{E}| = Blv} \tag{៤}$$

**Photo in source:** รូបទី២៤ shows the conducting-rod experimental setup with the rod on rails inside a magnetic field.

### Ohm's Law tie-in — Equation (៥)

If the circuit formed by the rod and its rails has total resistance $R$:

$$\boxed{I = \frac{|\mathcal{E}|}{R} = \frac{Blv}{R}} \tag{៥}$$

This is presented in the textbook in a dedicated `formulabox` titled **ច្បាប់អូម**.

## 5. Eddy Currents (ចរន្តភូកូល / ចរន្តអេឌី)

When a solid conductor (not just a wire loop) is placed in a changing magnetic field, **eddy currents** (ចរន្តភូកូល or ចរន្តអេឌី) swirl through its bulk. Two key consequences:

1. **Heating** — eddy currents dissipate energy as Joule heat ($P = I^2 R$), which is exploited in induction cooktops and induction furnaces.
2. **Lenz opposition** — because eddy currents create their own magnetic field opposing the change, they produce a **braking force** on any moving conductor passing through a field (used in magnetic braking systems on trains and amusement rides).

**Photo in source:** รូបទី២៧ illustrates eddy currents in a conductor with induced swirling current loops shown.

<Tip>
To reduce unwanted eddy currents in transformer cores, the iron core is built from thin, electrically insulated **laminations**. Each lamination is too thin to sustain large eddy currents, so resistive losses are drastically reduced.
</Tip>

## 6. Electric Generator (ជនិតាអគ្គិសនី) — Equation (៦)

An electric generator consists of a rectangular coil of $N$ turns, cross-sectional area $A$, rotating at angular frequency $\omega$ inside a uniform magnetic field $B$. The magnetic flux through the coil varies as:

$$\Phi_B = BA\cos(\omega t)$$

Applying Faraday's law:

$$e(t) = -N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = NBAω\sin(\omega t)$$

$$\boxed{e(t) = NBAω\sin(\omega t)} \tag{៦}$$

The **peak (maximum) EMF** is:

$$E_{max} = E_m = NBA\omega$$

**Photo in source:** รូបទី២៨ shows the spinning-coil generator schematic with the coil at angle $\omega t$ to the field.

**Symbol glossary for the generator:**

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|--------|---------|------|
| $N$ | Number of turns | — |
| $B$ | Magnetic field strength | T |
| $A$ | Coil cross-sectional area | m² |
| $\omega$ | Angular frequency of rotation | rad/s |
| $E_{max}$ | Peak EMF | V |

## 7. Electric Motor (ម៉ូទ័រអគ្គិសនី)

An electric motor is the **reverse of a generator**: instead of mechanical rotation producing current, an external current in the same coil-plus-field setup produces a torque and hence mechanical rotation. The same geometry applies — same coil, same field — but now $\vec{F}_B = I\vec{L} \times \vec{B}$ drives the rotation rather than induction.

**Photo in source:** รូបទី២៩ illustrates the motor configuration with current direction and force vectors labelled.

<Note>
A real motor also acts as a generator (back-EMF) once it is spinning. The back-EMF opposes the supply voltage and limits the current — this is why a stalled motor draws far more current than a running one and can overheat.
</Note>
