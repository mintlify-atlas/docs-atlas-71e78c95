Lesson 3 is the most mathematically intensive lesson in Chapter 3, covering **self-inductance** (អូតូអាំងឌុចស្យុង) and its two fundamental circuit applications: the **RL circuit** (where a resistor and inductor interact) and the **LC circuit** (where an inductor and capacitor exchange energy in oscillation). Equations (७)–(२९) — the longest run of new equations in the book — are introduced here, built up through the Kirchhoff loop rule, separation of variables, and the analogy between LC oscillations and mechanical simple harmonic motion.

---

## 1. Mutual Inductance (ម្យូချូអាំងឌុចស្យុង) — Equations (७)–(१४)

### Two-coil system

When the current $i_1$ in coil 1 changes, it alters the flux $\Phi_{B2}$ threading coil 2, inducing an EMF. By Faraday's law:

$$e_2 = -N_2\frac{d\Phi_{B2}}{dt} \tag{७}$$

The total flux linkage through coil 2 is **proportional** to the current in coil 1:

$$N_2\Phi_2 = M_{21}\,i_1 \tag{८}$$

Differentiating and substituting into (७):

$$e_2 = -M_{21}\frac{di_1}{dt} \tag{९}$$

Symmetrically, current changes in coil 2 induce in coil 1:

$$N_1\Phi_1 = M_{12}\,i_2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad e_1 = -M_{12}\frac{di_2}{dt} \tag{११–१२}$$

**Symmetry:** It can be shown that $M_{12} = M_{21}$, so a single **mutual inductance** $M$ describes both directions:

$$M_{12} = M_{21} = M \tag{—}$$

**Units:** $[M] = \text{henry (H)} = \text{Wb/A} = \text{V·s/A}$

## 2. Self-Inductance (អូតូអាំងឌុចស្យុង) — Equations (१५)–(१८)

### Inductance from geometry

A changing current $i$ through a coil creates a changing flux through itself. The total flux linkage is proportional to the current:

$$N\Phi = Li \tag{१५}$$

For a solenoid of $N$ turns, length $l$, and cross-sectional area $A$:

$$\boxed{L = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 A}{l}} \tag{१६}$$

**Photo in source:** รូបទី៣៣ (`magneticfluxviasolenoid.png`) illustrates flux through a solenoid cross-section.

### Self-induced EMF

Differentiating (१५) and applying Faraday's law gives the **instantaneous self-induced EMF**:

$$\boxed{e = -L\frac{di}{dt}} \tag{१७}$$

For a finite time interval $\Delta t$ with current change $\Delta I$:

$$\boxed{\mathcal{E} = -L\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t}} \tag{१८}$$

<Note>
The negative sign means the self-induced EMF always **opposes the change** in its own current — an inductor resists sudden changes in current just as a capacitor resists sudden changes in voltage. Together this is why RL and LC circuits have characteristic time constants.
</Note>

## 3. Magnetic Energy Stored in an Inductor — Equation (२०)

The work done building current from $0$ to $I$ in an inductor (integrated from the power $p = Li\,di/dt$) is stored as magnetic energy in the field:

$$\boxed{E_L = \frac{1}{2}LI^2} \tag{२०}$$

**Symbol glossary:**

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|--------|---------|------|
| $E_L$ | Magnetic energy | J |
| $L$ | Inductance | H |
| $I$ | Current through inductor | A |

<Tip>
This is the magnetic analogue of the electric potential energy $E_C = \frac{1}{2}CV^2$ stored in a capacitor. The pair $(L, C)$ and $(E_L, E_C)$ are the key symmetry behind LC oscillations.
</Tip>

## 4. RL Circuit — Switch Closed (Current Growth) — Equation (२१)

### Setup and Kirchhoff loop rule

When a switch is closed in a series RL circuit with EMF source $\mathcal{E}$, resistor $R$, and inductor $L$:

$$-\mathcal{E} + V_L + V_R = 0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
-\mathcal{E} + L\frac{di}{dt} + Ri = 0$$

Rearranging and separating variables, then integrating (using the integration helper $\int \frac{dx}{a - bx} = -\frac{1}{b}\ln|a - bx|$):

$$\boxed{i(t) = I_m\!\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_{RL}}\right)} \tag{२१}$$

where the **steady-state current** is $I_m = \mathcal{E}/R$ and the **RL time constant** is:

$$\tau_{RL} = \frac{L}{R}$$

**TikZ figure in source:** รូបទី៣៦ — exponential charging curve with numerical data table at $t = \tau, 2\tau, \ldots, 5\tau$, annotated with key values.

<Accordion title="Physical interpretation of τ_RL">

At $t = \tau_{RL}$: the current has risen to $I_m(1 - e^{-1}) \approx 0.632\,I_m$ — about 63% of its final value.  
At $t = 5\tau_{RL}$: the current is within 1% of $I_m$, considered "fully established."  

A large $L/R$ ratio means a long time constant — the inductor strongly resists the growing current. A small $L/R$ ratio means the current rises quickly.

</Accordion>

## 5. RL Circuit — Switch Opened (Current Decay) — Equation (२२)

When the EMF source is disconnected and the inductor drives current through $R$, the Kirchhoff equation becomes:

$$Ri + L\frac{di}{dt} = 0$$

Separating variables and integrating gives the **exponential decay**:

$$\boxed{i(t) = I_m\,e^{-t/\tau_{RL}}} \tag{२२}$$

The same time constant $\tau_{RL} = L/R$ governs the decay.

## 6. LC Circuit — Charging Phase — Equations (२३)–(२४)

### Initial charge on capacitor

With the SPDT switch set to terminal $a$, the capacitor $C$ charges from EMF source $\mathcal{E}$ to:

$$Q_0 = C\mathcal{E} \tag{२३}$$

The electrostatic energy stored is:

$$\boxed{E_E = \frac{1}{2}C\mathcal{E}^2} \tag{२४}$$

**CircuitikZ figure in source:** รូបទី៣๙ — vector circuit diagram showing the SPDT switch at terminal $a$, capacitor, and initial conditions $q(0) = Q_0$, $i(0) = 0$.

## 7. LC Circuit — Oscillation Phase — Equations (२५)–(२८)

### Kirchhoff loop equation

When the switch moves to terminal $b$, the capacitor is isolated with the inductor. With the sign convention $i = -dq/dt$ (capacitor discharges):

$$-v_C + v_L = 0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
-\frac{q}{C} + L\frac{di}{dt} = 0$$

Substituting $i = -dq/dt$:

$$\boxed{\frac{d^2q}{dt^2} + \frac{1}{LC}q = 0} \tag{२५}$$

This is identical in form to the SHM equation $\ddot{x} + \omega^2 x = 0$, confirming LC oscillations are the electromagnetic analogue of a mass-spring system.

**CircuitikZ figure in source:** รូปที่๔๐ — SPDT switch at terminal $b$ showing the closed LC loop.

### General solution

The charge oscillates as:

$$\boxed{q(t) = q_{max}\cos(\omega t + \varphi)} \tag{२६}$$

with angular frequency $\omega = 1/\sqrt{LC}$.

The instantaneous current:

$$\boxed{i(t) = -I_{max}\sin(\omega t + \varphi)} \tag{२७}$$

where $I_{max} = \omega\,q_{max}$.

### Natural period

$$\boxed{\tau_{LC} = T = 2\pi\sqrt{LC}} \tag{२८}$$

**TikZ figure in source:** รูบที่๔๑ — dual-subplot vector figure plotting charge $q(t)$ (brownheader colour) and current $i(t)$ (primary colour) on separate axes, with vertical dashed alignment lines at $T/4$, $T/2$, $3T/4$, $T$ illustrating the 90° phase offset between $q$ and $i$.

<Accordion title="Analogy: LC oscillator ↔ mass-spring oscillator">

| LC circuit | Mass-spring system |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Charge $q$ | Displacement $x$ |
| Current $i = dq/dt$ | Velocity $v = dx/dt$ |
| Inductance $L$ | Mass $m$ |
| $1/C$ | Spring constant $k$ |
| $\omega = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ | $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ |
| Magnetic energy $\frac{1}{2}Li^2$ | Kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$ |
| Electric energy $\frac{1}{2}q^2/C$ | Potential energy $\frac{1}{2}kx^2$ |

</Accordion>

## 8. Total Electromagnetic Energy — Equation (२९)

In an ideal (lossless) LC circuit, energy shuttles between the inductor's magnetic field and the capacitor's electric field with no dissipation. The total energy is constant:

$$\boxed{E = E_L + E_C = \frac{1}{2}Li^2 + \frac{1}{2}Cv^2 = \text{constant}} \tag{२९}$$

Evaluated at extreme points:

$$E = \frac{1}{2}LI_{max}^2 = \frac{Q_0^2}{2C} = \frac{1}{2}C\mathcal{E}^2$$

<Note>
In a real circuit, resistance dissipates energy and the oscillations are **damped**. The LC model is the ideal limit $R \to 0$. Including a small resistance leads to the damped RLC oscillator, which is the topic of later lessons.
</Note>
