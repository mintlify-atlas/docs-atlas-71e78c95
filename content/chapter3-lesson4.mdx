## 1. Historical Context: The War of Currents (1880s)

The opening of Lesson 4 sets the scene with one of the most consequential engineering debates in history. In the **1880s**, two competing visions for electrical power distribution clashed:

| Camp | Figure | Technology |
|------|--------|-----------|
| **DC (Direct Current)** | Thomas Edison | Constant-direction current; each neighbourhood needed its own generator |
| **AC (Alternating Current)** | George Westinghouse | Sinusoidal current; voltage can be stepped up or down with a transformer |

Edison championed direct current and had already built the world's first commercial power station (Pearl Street, New York, 1882). Westinghouse, backed by Nikola Tesla's polyphase AC system, argued that alternating current could be transmitted over far longer distances at far lower cost.

The debate was fierce — Edison staged public demonstrations of AC dangers — but the engineering and economic arguments overwhelmingly favoured AC. By the mid-1890s, the **Niagara Falls hydroelectric project** (using Westinghouse/Tesla AC) settled the matter. AC became the global standard for power distribution, a position it still holds today.

## 2. Why AC Won: Sinusoidal Waveform and Transformers

The decisive advantage of alternating current lies in two related facts:

1. **Sinusoidal waveform** — the voltage and current in an AC system vary smoothly as a sine function in time. This smooth periodicity makes AC compatible with rotating machinery (generators and motors produce sinusoidal output naturally) and with the mathematics of inductance and capacitance.

2. **Transformers (ត្រង់ស្វូ)** — a transformer can efficiently step AC voltage up or down using electromagnetic induction. There is no practical equivalent for DC.

### Why transformers matter for power transmission

Power lost as heat in a transmission line is $P_{loss} = I^2 R_{line}$. To minimise loss over long distances, we want **low current** — which means **high voltage** (since $P = IV$ is fixed by the power plant's output). A step-up transformer raises voltage (and lowers current) for transmission; a step-down transformer at the far end reduces voltage to safe levels for homes and factories.

The turns-ratio formula relating primary and secondary voltages and turn counts, along with its derivation from Faraday's law, will be introduced formally when the full Lesson 4 equations are finalised.

### DC cannot be transformed

A DC voltage is constant in time — it produces a constant magnetic flux in the transformer core, and a **constant** flux induces **zero** EMF (Faraday's law: $\mathcal{E} = -N\,d\Phi/dt$, and $d\Phi/dt = 0$ for DC). Transformers simply do not work with direct current, which is why Edison's DC distribution network required generating stations every mile or so in a city.

## 4. Connection to Previous Lessons

Lesson 4 draws directly on the groundwork laid in Lessons 2 and 3:

- The **generator equation** $e(t) = NBA\omega\sin(\omega t)$ from Lesson 2 is the origin of the sinusoidal AC waveform — a generator spinning at constant $\omega$ naturally produces AC.
- The **self-inductance** $L$ and the **LC oscillation** period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{LC}$ from Lesson 3 are the building blocks of AC circuit reactance and resonance.
- The **transformer** introduced here rests entirely on **Faraday's law** (Lesson 2): mutual induction between a primary and secondary coil is what makes voltage transformation possible.
